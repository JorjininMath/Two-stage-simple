% ===========================================================================
\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================

We study adaptive bandwidth selection $h(x)$ for the smooth indicator inside
the CKME conditional CDF estimator, when used as the score function in split
conformal prediction. The standard practice fixes $h$ at a single CV-tuned
scalar; we ask whether locally-adaptive $h(x)$ improves \emph{conditional}
performance (interval score, group-conditional coverage) without breaking
marginal validity.

The report has two parts:
\begin{description}
  \item[Oracle ($\S$\ref{sec:oracle}).] When the per-$x$ noise scale $s(x)$
  is known, set $h(x) = c \cdot s(x)$. We establish marginal validity, paired
  improvement in interval score, and the role of $c$ in the
  fairness--width trade-off.
  \item[Plug-in ($\S$\ref{sec:plugin}).] When $s(x)$ is unknown, estimate it
  from Stage~1 raw data. We use a robust scale (IQR$/1.349$) smoothed by
  Nadaraya--Watson regression. We quantify the gap to the oracle and verify
  the estimator's behavior under heavy-tailed (Student-$t_3$) noise.
\end{description}

% ===========================================================================
\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Pipeline}

Across both parts we use a fixed two-stage pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Train CKME on $D_0 = \{(x_i, y_{ij})\}$ with $n_0$ sites and $r_0$
  replicates per site (Stage~1).
  \item Calibrate split conformal on $D_1$ collected at $n_1$ Stage-2 sites
  with $r_1$ replicates each.
  \item Evaluate on $n_{\mathrm{test}}$ test points.
\end{enumerate}
Hyperparameters $(\ell_x, \lambda, h)$ are CV-tuned per DGP on a pilot dataset
(\texttt{exp\_adaptive\_h/pretrain\_params.py}). Pre-experiment sensitivity
analysis confirmed $h$-insensitivity on a finer grid; the reported values
hit the lower edge of the CV grid but the experiments were verified
insensitive to further reduction.

\subsection{Adaptive bandwidth evaluation}

The CKME coefficients $C(x) = (K_X + \lambda I)^{-1} k_x$ depend only on the
$x$-kernel; the bandwidth $h$ enters only through the smooth indicator
$g_{t,h}$. We can therefore swap a per-query bandwidth $h(x_*)$ at evaluation
time without retraining: the conditional CDF estimate becomes
\[
  \widehat{F}_h(t \mid x_*) = \langle C(x_*),\ g_{t,h(x_*)}(Y) \rangle.
\]
Calibration recomputes $\hat{q}$ using $h(x_i)$ at each calibration point;
prediction inverts the level set on a $t$-grid using $h(x_*)$.

\subsection{Data-generating processes}

Four 1D DGPs span Gaussian/non-Gaussian and homoscedastic/heteroscedastic regimes:
\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{wsc\_gauss}.] Smooth heteroscedastic Gaussian, scale
  $s(x) = 0.01 + 0.20(x-\pi)^2$.
  \item[\texttt{gibbs\_s1}.] Heteroscedastic Gaussian, $s(x) = |\sin x|$.
  Interior zero of $s$ at $x = 0, \pi$.
  \item[\texttt{exp1}.] M/G/1 queue, $x \in [0.1, 0.9]$, boundary explosion
  via Pollaczek--Khinchine.
  \item[\texttt{nongauss\_A1L}.] Student-$t_3$ noise with scale
  $s(x) = 0.01 + 0.20(x-\pi)^2$. Heavy-tailed; $\mathrm{SD}(Y \mid x) = s(x)\sqrt{3}$.
\end{description}

\subsection{Metrics}

Marginal coverage $\hat{P}(Y \in [\hat L, \hat U])$ targets $1-\alpha = 0.9$.
Conditional fairness uses bin-wise coverage on equal-frequency $x$-bins,
reported as worst-bin and mean-bin $|c_{\mathrm{bin}} - (1-\alpha)|$.
Width is $\mathbb{E}[U-L]$; the Winkler interval score
$\mathrm{IS}(L,U,Y) = (U-L) + (2/\alpha)(L-Y)_+ + (2/\alpha)(Y-U)_+$ combines
both.

\section{Oracle adaptive bandwidth}\label{sec:oracle}
% ===========================================================================

We set $h(x) = c \cdot s(x)$ with the per-DGP oracle $s(x)$ defined above
(for \texttt{nongauss\_A1L}, $s(x)$ is the Student-$t$ \emph{scale}, not its
SD). Floors $h(x) \ge 10^{-3}$ avoid degenerate indicators where $s(x) = 0$.

across all four DGPs (Table~\ref{tab:exp1}). Empirical coverage lies within
0.005 of the nominal 0.9 in every cell.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Plug-in adaptive bandwidth}\label{sec:plugin}
% ===========================================================================

When $s(x)$ is unknown we replace it with a data-driven estimator built from
Stage~1 raw data, and ask: how close to oracle performance can we get?

\subsection{Estimator}\label{sec:plugin-est}

For each Stage~1 site $x_i$, we compute a robust per-site scale
\[
  \hat\sigma_i \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{IQR}(Y_{i,1}, \ldots, Y_{i,r_0})}{1.349},
\]
where $1.349 = \Phi^{-1}(0.75) - \Phi^{-1}(0.25)$ ensures Fisher consistency
to $\sigma$ under Gaussian noise (Silverman, 1986; Hampel, 1974).
The point estimates $(x_i, \hat\sigma_i)$ are then smoothed by Nadaraya--Watson
regression with a Gaussian kernel and Silverman bandwidth, yielding a
callable $\hat\sigma(\cdot)$. The plug-in bandwidth is
$h_{\mathrm{plug}}(x) = c \cdot \hat\sigma(x)$, floored at $10^{-3}$.

\paragraph{Why IQR rather than sample SD?} Under Student-$t_\nu$ noise with
$\nu \le 4$, the sample variance has \emph{infinite variance}: convergence
of the SD estimator is dominated by the heaviest replicate. The robust scale
$\mathrm{IQR}/1.349$ is quantile-based and has finite-variance asymptotics
for any $\nu > 0$ (Hampel, 1974; Rousseeuw and Croux, 1993). In particular,
for symmetric $t_\nu$ with scale $s$,
\[
  \frac{\mathrm{IQR}/1.349}{s} \xrightarrow{p} \kappa_\nu
  \;:=\; \frac{q_{0.75}(t_\nu) - q_{0.25}(t_\nu)}{1.349},
\]
which equals $1$ in the Gaussian limit and $\kappa_3 \approx 1.134$ for
$\nu = 3$.

\paragraph{Finite-$r_0$ bias.} The $r_0$ replicates per site are small
($r_0 = 10$ in our setup), and the empirical IQR computed from $r_0$ samples
is itself biased: a Monte-Carlo evaluation of the linear-interpolation
quantile estimator at $r_0 = 10$ gives
$\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{IQR}_{r_0}/1.349] \approx 0.87 \cdot \sigma$ under
Gaussian noise (i.e., a $\approx\!13\%$ shrinkage), and approximately the
same multiplicative factor under $t_3$. The plug-in scale therefore tracks
$c_{r_0}\!\cdot\!\sigma(x)$ rather than $\sigma(x)$ itself, where
$c_{r_0} \to 1$ as $r_0 \to \infty$. As we show below, conformal calibration
absorbs this constant offset into $\hat q$.

\subsection{Gap to oracle}\label{sec:plugin-gap}

Across budgets $B = n_0 r_0 \in \{50, 100, 250, 500\}$ with $r_0 = 10$ fixed,
the absolute gap $|\mathrm{cov}_{\mathrm{plug}} - \mathrm{cov}_{\mathrm{oracle}}|$
is already at the macrorep noise floor at the smallest $B = 50$, with no
clear monotone decay across larger $B$ (Figure~\ref{fig:exp4a}). The
practical reading is that conformal calibration absorbs estimation error in
$\hat\sigma(\cdot)$ at this budget regime; the Gap-Theorem bound is loose
relative to observed gaps.

\subsection{Behavior under heavy-tailed noise}\label{sec:plugin-tails}

The ratio $\bar h_{\mathrm{plug}}(x) / \bar h_{\mathrm{oracle}}(x)$ has two
sources of mismatch with the oracle scale: (i) the population-level constant
$\kappa_\nu$ from $\S$\ref{sec:plugin-est} ($\kappa_2 = 1$,
$\kappa_3 \approx 1.134$), and (ii) the finite-$r_0$ shrinkage
$c_{r_0} \approx 0.87$. The combined target is therefore
$c_{r_0} \cdot \kappa_\nu$: $\approx\! 0.87$ for the Gaussian DGPs and
$\approx\! 0.99$ for $t_3$.

Empirically, at the largest budget $B = 500$, the IQR plug-in tracks these
predictions (Figure~\ref{fig:exp4b}, Table~\ref{tab:exp4-ablation}):
$\bar h_{\mathrm{p}}/\bar h_{\mathrm{o}} \in \{0.85, 0.88, 0.84\}$ for the
three Gaussian DGPs and $0.96$ for \texttt{nongauss\_A1L}. By contrast,
a sample-SD plug-in shows a $\approx\!1.42$ ratio under $t_3$ that decreases
slowly in $B$ but never reaches its formal target $\sqrt 3 \approx 1.73$,
reflecting the infinite-variance asymptotics of sample variance for
$\nu \le 4$. Despite the IQR offset, downstream coverage and interval-score
metrics remain close to the oracle: the bin-wise coverage curves of the
plug-in and oracle arms overlap to plotting precision across all four DGPs
(Figure~\ref{fig:exp4b-cov}), confirming that the constant rescaling of
$h$ is absorbed into $\hat q$ by conformal calibration.

\subsection{Ablation: sample SD vs.\ robust IQR estimator}\label{sec:plugin-ablation}

We rerun the full Exp4 sweep ($K = 50$ macroreps, common random numbers)
with the scale estimator swapped from $\mathrm{IQR}/1.349$ to
$\mathrm{std}(Y_{i,\cdot})$ and report results at $B = 500$ in
Table~\ref{tab:exp4-ablation}. Both estimators yield the same plug-in
coverage to within $\le 0.001$, and the same coverage-gap distribution,
reaffirming that conformal calibration absorbs scale offsets. The two
estimators differ in their bandwidth-ratio behavior: sample-SD is closer
to $1$ on Gaussian DGPs (no $\kappa_\nu$ offset) but produces an unstable
$1.42$ ratio on $t_3$ (target $\sqrt 3$, not reached due to infinite-variance
asymptotics). $\mathrm{IQR}/1.349$ shows a uniform $\approx\!0.87$
finite-$r_0$ shrinkage (predicted by $\S$\ref{sec:plugin-est}) and matches
its theoretical $t_3$ target within finite-sample noise. We default to
$\mathrm{IQR}/1.349$ because it has finite-variance asymptotics for any
$\nu > 0$ and a well-defined population limit even under heavy tails.

\section{Discussion}
% ===========================================================================

Three findings stand out. First, replacing a CV-tuned scalar $h$ with an
oracle $h(x) = c \cdot s(x)$ is a strict improvement on conditional
performance: bin-wise worst deviation drops on every DGP and oracle wins
the paired interval score in $98$--$100\%$ of macroreps, while marginal
validity is preserved
($\hat P(\text{cov}) - 0.9 < 0.005$ in every cell, $\S$\ref{sec:oracle}).

Second, the plug-in $\hat\sigma(\cdot)$ built from Stage~1 raw data
recovers the bulk of oracle's gain. The coverage gap to oracle is at the
macrorep noise floor already at $B = 50$ and shows no monotone decay across
larger $B$ ($\S$\ref{sec:plugin-gap}); for the budgets and DGPs studied
here, the practical bottleneck is the calibration sample, not the scale
estimator.

Third, the scale estimator's behavior decomposes cleanly into a
population-level limit and a finite-$r_0$ shrinkage. For
$\mathrm{IQR}/1.349$, the population limit is $1$ on Gaussian noise and
$\kappa_3 \approx 1.134$ on $t_3$; the finite-$r_0$ shrinkage at $r_0 = 10$
is a $\approx\!13\%$ multiplicative factor that applies uniformly across
DGPs. Conformal calibration absorbs this constant rescaling, which is why
SD and IQR plug-ins yield indistinguishable coverage and IS even though
their bandwidth ratios differ ($\S$\ref{sec:plugin-ablation}). The IQR
estimator is preferable on theoretical grounds (finite-variance
asymptotics for any $\nu > 0$), and is empirically immaterial on Gaussian
benchmarks where SD is also valid.
